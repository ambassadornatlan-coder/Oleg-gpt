1. Executive Summary

Aircraft navigation and tracking systems operate in environments where motion regimes, sensor quality, and external disturbances can change abruptly. Examples include transitions between straight flight and maneuvering, wind gusts, sensor dropouts, sudden measurement biases, and navigation-system degradation.

A useful mathematical framework combines three elements:

  • Markov-switching dynamics to represent discrete changes in operating mode;
  • Generalized functions and distributions to describe idealized impulsive inputs and discontinuities in a mathematically precise way;
  • Probabilistic state estimation to infer the aircraft state and, where possible, the active mode from noisy measurements.
Central finding. Generalized functions provide a useful representation of impulsive disturbances, but they do not, by themselves, solve the state-estimation problem. Their value comes from being integrated into a well-defined hybrid dynamic model and an estimator that accounts for mode uncertainty, measurement noise, and possible state jumps.

Interacting Multiple Model (IMM) filters are an established approach to maneuvering-target tracking. Kalman-family filters, robust filtering methods, and switching estimators offer complementary tools. A key research challenge is to combine mode switching and discontinuous disturbances without double-counting their effects or introducing mathematically undefined operations.

This report is a targeted literature synthesis and methodological analysis, not a systematic review or a claim that a new algorithm has been experimentally validated.

2. Scope and Methodology

2.1 Research question

How can generalized functions be incorporated into the modeling and estimation of dynamic systems with Markovian mode switching and discontinuous disturbances, particularly for aircraft navigation and tracking?

2.2 Main research dimensions

  1. Mathematical representation of impulses and discontinuous signals.
  2. State-space models with discrete operating modes.
  3. Bayesian mode estimation and state filtering.
  4. Integration with inertial navigation and satellite-based positioning.
  5. Robustness, observability, numerical implementation, and experimental validation.

2.3 Evidence and limitations

The references below cover foundational distribution theory, Markov jump-system filtering, IMM-based maneuvering-target estimation, and navigation applications. The review is targeted: it does not establish exhaustive coverage of all relevant publications, and individual claims should be checked against the full text of the cited papers before being used in a formal publication or thesis.

No independent research agents, executable simulations, or experimental flight tests are claimed in this report. The proposed research workflow is a methodological recommendation rather than a reported experimental result.

3. Problem Formulation

3.1 Continuous state and discrete mode

Let \(x(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\) denote the continuous state vector, \(u(t)\) the control input, and \(r(t)\in\{1,\ldots,M\}\) a discrete operating mode. The mode may represent a flight regime, a sensor-health condition, or a model associated with a particular maneuver.

A general hybrid model can be written as

\[ \dot{x}(t)=A_{r(t)}x(t)+B_{r(t)}u(t)+f_{r(t)}(x(t),u(t),t)+d(t). \]

Here, \(A_{r(t)}\) and \(B_{r(t)}\) depend on the active mode, \(f_{r(t)}\) represents possible nonlinear dynamics, and \(d(t)\) represents external disturbances. This expression is a modeling template; the particular matrices and disturbance assumptions depend on the application.

3.2 Measurements

At discrete observation times \(t_k\), measurements can be represented as

\[ y_k=H_{r_k}x_k+v_k, \]

where \(H_{r_k}\) is the mode-dependent observation matrix and \(v_k\) is measurement noise. For nonlinear navigation sensors, a more general observation model is \(y_k=h_{r_k}(x_k)+v_k\).

3.3 Markov mode switching

The discrete mode is commonly modeled as a Markov chain. For a time-homogeneous chain, its transition probabilities are

\[ \pi_{ij}=\Pr(r_k=j\mid r_{k-1}=i), \qquad \sum_{j=1}^{M}\pi_{ij}=1. \]

The Markov assumption means that the next mode depends on the current mode, not directly on the entire preceding mode history. This is a modeling assumption and may be inadequate when mode transitions depend on flight phase, control commands, or other context. In those cases, a context-dependent transition matrix may be more appropriate.

3.4 Why discontinuities matter

A conventional smooth disturbance model may not represent a sudden event efficiently. A step change, a short high-amplitude input, or a sudden sensor bias can cause abrupt changes in the state or residuals. A model that ignores these events may produce delayed maneuver detection, biased estimates, or an overconfident uncertainty estimate.

4. Generalized Functions and Distribution Theory

4.1 The Dirac delta distribution

The Dirac delta is not an ordinary function with a finite value at one point. It is a distribution defined by its action on a suitable test function \(\varphi\):

\[ \langle \delta(t-\tau),\varphi(t)\rangle=\varphi(\tau). \]

The delta distribution idealizes an event concentrated at time \(\tau\). Its integral over an interval containing \(\tau\) is one:

\[ \int_{\tau-\varepsilon}^{\tau+\varepsilon} \delta(t-\tau)\,dt=1,\qquad \varepsilon>0. \]

In engineering, an impulse with magnitude \(a\) is often represented as \(a\delta(t-\tau)\). The parameter \(a\) describes the impulse's integrated magnitude, not its finite instantaneous amplitude.

4.2 Step functions and their derivatives

The Heaviside step function \(H(t-\tau)\) represents a signal that changes at time \(\tau\). In the distributional sense,

\[ \frac{d}{dt}H(t-\tau)=\delta(t-\tau). \]

This identity is useful when describing abrupt changes in an input or bias. It should not be interpreted as an ordinary pointwise derivative at the discontinuity.

4.3 Impulsive disturbances and state jumps

Consider a linear system subject to an idealized impulse:

\[ \dot{x}(t)=Ax(t)+Bu(t)+Ea\,\delta(t-\tau). \]

Integrating over a small interval around \(\tau\) gives

\[ x(\tau^+)-x(\tau^-) = \int_{\tau^-}^{\tau^+}Ax(t)\,dt + \int_{\tau^-}^{\tau^+}Bu(t)\,dt + Ea. \]

If the ordinary terms remain bounded and integrable over the shrinking interval, their contributions tend to zero. Therefore, the state jump is

\[ \boxed{x(\tau^+)=x(\tau^-)+Ea.} \]

This result holds under the stated assumptions. The matrix \(E\) maps the impulse magnitude into the state coordinates. It is especially important to distinguish a physical state jump from a discontinuity in a measured signal or in a sensor bias.

4.4 Distributional modeling is not a universal cure

Generalized functions provide a rigorous way to describe certain singular inputs, but products of distributions are not automatically defined. For example, multiplying a delta distribution by another discontinuous object at the same point may require additional modeling conventions. A proposed model must specify how the event acts on the state and how the state is propagated across the event.

4.5 Practical alternatives

  • Finite-duration pulse: use a high-amplitude input over a short interval when the physical event has nonzero duration.
  • Jump map: represent the event explicitly as \(x^+=J(x^-,a)\).
  • Step-change model: use a Heaviside-type signal for a persistent change, such as a sensor bias.
  • Heavy-tailed noise: use a suitable non-Gaussian model when disturbances are large but not genuinely impulsive.

The choice should reflect the physical mechanism, not merely mathematical convenience.

5. Markov-Switching Dynamic Models

5.1 Markov jump linear systems

A discrete-time Markov jump linear system can be written as

\[ x_{k+1}=A_{r_k}x_k+B_{r_k}u_k+w_k, \qquad y_k=H_{r_k}x_k+v_k. \]

The state-transition matrix changes with the discrete mode. This structure is suitable for representing several approximately linear flight or sensor regimes. The process and measurement noise assumptions should be stated explicitly; Gaussian noise is convenient but is not always realistic.

5.2 Adding discontinuous events

A model with a possible jump at observation step \(k\) can be represented as

\[ x_{k+1}=A_{r_k}x_k+B_{r_k}u_k+w_k+E_{r_k}a_k, \]

where \(a_k\) may be zero most of the time and nonzero when a jump event occurs. This discrete-time form does not require numerical integration of a delta distribution. It can be useful when the estimator operates at a fixed sampling rate.

A separate event indicator \(q_k\) may be introduced to distinguish ordinary process noise from rare events:

\[ q_k\in\{0,1\},\qquad x_{k+1}=A_{r_k}x_k+B_{r_k}u_k+w_k+q_kE_{r_k}a_k. \]

The joint model of \(r_k\) and \(q_k\) must be designed carefully. Assuming that mode switches and impulses are independent may be convenient, but can be physically implausible when impulses are associated with particular maneuvers or sensor failures.

5.3 Hybrid event timing

An event may occur between two sensor observations. If its exact time is unknown, the estimator must account for uncertainty about both the event magnitude and its timing. A practical implementation may use an event-time hypothesis, a latent event indicator, or a finite-duration pulse model. The most appropriate choice depends on the sampling rate and available sensors.

6. State Estimation Methods

6.1 Bayesian filtering framework

The goal is to estimate the hidden state and mode from the observation history \(Y_k=\{y_1,\ldots,y_k\}\). Conceptually, the estimator seeks

\[ p(x_k,r_k\mid Y_k). \]

The full joint posterior can be computationally expensive. Many practical filters approximate it by maintaining a small number of mode-conditioned state estimates and probabilities.

6.2 Interacting Multiple Model (IMM) filtering

An IMM filter runs a bank of filters, each associated with a different motion model or operating mode. The filters exchange information through probabilistic mixing. The algorithm is widely used in maneuvering-target tracking because it can respond to changes in motion without requiring one single model to describe every maneuver.

The mode-probability prediction is

\[ \bar{\mu}_{k}^{(j)} = \sum_{i=1}^{M}\pi_{ij}\mu_{k-1}^{(i)}. \]

Here, \(\mu_{k-1}^{(i)}\) is the previous posterior probability of mode \(i\). After receiving a measurement, the probability of mode \(j\) is updated using its likelihood:

\[ \mu_k^{(j)} = \frac{\Lambda_k^{(j)}\bar{\mu}_k^{(j)}} {\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\Lambda_k^{(\ell)}\bar{\mu}_k^{(\ell)}}. \]

The likelihood \(\Lambda_k^{(j)}\) is computed from the measurement residual and its predicted uncertainty under model \(j\). This formula assumes the denominator is nonzero and the likelihoods are appropriately defined.

6.3 Kalman-family filters

  • Kalman filter: appropriate for linear dynamics with compatible noise assumptions.
  • Extended Kalman filter (EKF): uses local linearization for nonlinear models.
  • Unscented Kalman filter (UKF): propagates selected sigma points to approximate nonlinear transformations.
  • IMM-EKF or IMM-UKF: combines mode uncertainty with nonlinear state estimation.

No single variant is universally superior. Performance depends on the degree of nonlinearity, sampling frequency, measurement geometry, process-noise specification, and computational budget.

6.4 Robust and non-Gaussian filtering

Abrupt disturbances can create residuals inconsistent with a Gaussian noise model. Possible responses include robust loss functions, innovation-based outlier detection, heavy-tailed noise models, adaptive covariance estimation, and explicit jump hypotheses.

H-infinity filtering offers a robustness-oriented alternative under a specified uncertainty formulation. Correntropy-based methods have also been studied for navigation processing in non-Gaussian environments. These approaches address different assumptions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

6.5 Explicit event estimation

A filter that only increases process-noise covariance may become less confident after an abrupt event, but it may not estimate the event itself. If the impulse magnitude is important, augment the state or introduce a separate event estimator. For example,

\[ z_k= \begin{bmatrix} x_k\\ a_k \end{bmatrix}, \]

where \(a_k\) represents an unknown event magnitude or a slowly varying disturbance parameter. Such augmentation requires suitable dynamics or priors for \(a_k\), as well as sufficient observability to distinguish it from changes in the aircraft state.

7. Relevance to Aircraft Navigation

7.1 Navigation state variables

A navigation state may include position, velocity, attitude, inertial sensor biases, and additional parameters. A simplified vector is

\[ x_k= \begin{bmatrix} p_k & v_k & \theta_k & b_{g,k} & b_{a,k} \end{bmatrix}^{T}, \]

where \(p_k\) denotes position, \(v_k\) velocity, \(\theta_k\) an attitude parameterization, and \(b_g,b_a\) gyroscope and accelerometer biases. The actual state representation depends on the navigation architecture and coordinate convention.

7.2 Inertial and satellite navigation integration

In an integrated inertial-navigation and GNSS system, inertial measurements support high-rate propagation, while GNSS provides external position and velocity information when available. A mode-switching model can represent different operating conditions, such as:

  • nominal GNSS availability;
  • degraded or intermittent GNSS measurements;
  • maneuvering flight;
  • suspected sensor bias or corruption;
  • recovery after an abnormal measurement interval.

An estimator should distinguish changes in vehicle motion from changes in sensor behavior. Otherwise, it may interpret a measurement fault as a physical maneuver, or vice versa.

7.3 Disturbances relevant to aircraft

Potential events include rapid wind changes, abrupt changes in measured acceleration, transient sensor faults, data dropouts, and sudden bias shifts. Not all of these are physically impulsive. For example, a wind gust usually has a finite time profile; an ideal delta input is an approximation that may be appropriate for analysis but not for all flight-control simulations.

7.4 What the combined framework can offer

  • Faster detection of changes in motion regime when the mode models are well chosen.
  • More explicit representation of rare state changes or sensor disturbances.
  • Improved diagnostic information through mode probabilities and event estimates.
  • A principled way to compare smooth-noise models with jump-aware models.

These are potential benefits, not guaranteed performance gains. They must be demonstrated against appropriate baselines using realistic scenarios and independent test data.

9. Proposed Research Workflow

Step 1 — Define the physical event classes

List the events to be modeled and determine whether each is best represented as a mode change, a state jump, a sensor fault, a finite-duration disturbance, or ordinary stochastic noise. Avoid using a delta distribution merely because an observed signal changes rapidly.

Step 2 — Build a baseline navigation model

Specify the state vector, coordinate frames, sampling rates, control inputs, sensor models, noise assumptions, and mode-transition probabilities. Document the assumptions that make the model observable and numerically tractable.

Step 3 — Define the discontinuity mathematically

Choose between a continuous-time distributional input and a discrete-time jump map. State the jump matrix or nonlinear reset function, the event magnitude model, and any dependence between the event and the current operating mode.

Step 4 — Implement baseline estimators

Start with a single-model Kalman-family filter and an IMM estimator. Add a jump-aware or robust estimator as a separate model variant. This makes it possible to identify which component provides any observed benefit.

Step 5 — Design controlled scenarios

  • Nominal flight with no abrupt events.
  • Known maneuver transitions.
  • Impulse-like or short-duration disturbances of varied magnitude.
  • GNSS outage, delayed measurement, or corrupted observation.
  • Combined maneuver and sensor-fault scenarios.
  • Incorrect mode-transition probabilities and mismatched noise assumptions.

Step 6 — Evaluate consistently

Useful metrics include position and velocity RMSE, attitude error, mode-classification accuracy, event detection delay, false-alarm rate, recovery time, and computational cost. Uncertainty calibration should also be evaluated: a filter should not only produce accurate estimates but should represent its confidence credibly.

\[ \operatorname{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} \left\|\hat{x}_k-x_k^{\mathrm{true}}\right\|^2}. \]

The formula above is a generic state-error metric. In practice, position, velocity, and attitude should often be evaluated separately because their units and scales differ.

Step 7 — Test generalization

Tune the estimator on one set of scenarios and test it on different trajectories, disturbance magnitudes, noise levels, and mode-transition patterns. Use Monte Carlo repetitions to estimate variability rather than reporting a single favorable run.

Step 8 — Validate against physical constraints

Check whether estimated trajectories, accelerations, and attitude changes remain physically plausible. For any proposed operational use, simulation results alone are insufficient: validation must reflect the intended hardware, sensor characteristics, and safety requirements.

10. Limitations and Technical Challenges

10.1 Identifiability

A sudden residual may be explained by several mechanisms: a true maneuver, an external disturbance, a sensor bias jump, or a faulty measurement. If the available sensors cannot distinguish these explanations, the estimator may not uniquely identify the cause. Additional sensors, physical constraints, or informative priors may be necessary.

10.2 Model-bank design

An IMM filter only performs as well as the models it can represent. Too few models may fail to capture important behavior; too many may increase computational cost and create redundant hypotheses. Transition probabilities should reflect plausible switching behavior and should be subjected to sensitivity analysis.

10.3 Numerical approximation

A continuous-time impulse must be handled carefully in numerical integration. Replacing it with an arbitrarily high finite pulse can make results dependent on the integration step. An explicit jump update is often more stable when the physical model supports it.

10.4 Inconsistent probability assumptions

A model that assumes independent mode switching, impulse occurrence, and sensor failures may be convenient but can be inaccurate. Dependence should be modeled when supported by the physical process or the available data.

10.5 Evidence quality

Published results may use different datasets, sensor configurations, trajectories, disturbance models, and performance metrics. Direct comparisons across papers can therefore be misleading. A fair evaluation requires a common experimental protocol and transparent reporting.

11. Future Research Directions

11.1 Joint mode and event estimation

Develop an estimator that jointly estimates the continuous state, Markov mode, and the presence or magnitude of a discontinuous event. The central question is whether the added event model improves estimation without creating excessive false alarms or computational overhead.

11.2 Adaptive transition probabilities

Investigate whether mode-transition probabilities can be adjusted using flight context, control inputs, or innovation statistics. Any adaptive rule should be tested for stability and resistance to spurious switching.

11.3 Physically informed disturbance models

Compare ideal impulses with finite-duration gust models and sensor-fault models. This can clarify when generalized functions provide a meaningful approximation and when a more detailed physical description is required.

11.4 Robustness and uncertainty calibration

Evaluate robust filters and jump-aware IMM variants under outliers, model mismatch, and sensor degradation. Include confidence calibration and false-alarm metrics, not only mean state error.

11.5 Efficient real-time implementation

Explore event-triggered updates, reduced model banks, pruning of low-probability hypotheses, and fixed-cost approximations. Any efficiency claim should be supported by timing measurements on the intended computing platform.

11.6 Reproducibility

A strong study should provide explicit equations, parameter tables, initial conditions, transition matrices, event distributions, code, and test scenarios where sharing is permitted. Reproducibility is especially important because performance can depend heavily on estimator tuning.

12. Conclusions

Markov-switching models and generalized functions address complementary aspects of aircraft navigation under changing operating conditions. Markov models describe uncertainty about the active dynamic regime, while generalized functions provide a rigorous language for idealized impulsive inputs and discontinuities. State-estimation algorithms connect these models to noisy observations.

IMM-based filtering is a well-established starting point for maneuvering-target estimation. For navigation problems involving abrupt disturbances, a promising research direction is to compare conventional IMM filtering with an explicitly jump-aware or event-augmented variant. Robust filtering methods provide additional comparison points when the main challenge is uncertainty or non-Gaussian measurement corruption.

The main methodological requirement is to define the physical meaning of each discontinuity before choosing its mathematical representation. An ideal impulse, a persistent step change, a maneuver transition, and a sensor fault are different phenomena and should not be conflated.

Recommended core experiment: compare a baseline Kalman-family filter, an IMM estimator, and a jump-aware IMM estimator on the same simulated navigation scenarios. Measure state accuracy, mode classification, event detection delay, false alarms, uncertainty calibration, and computational cost. This experiment would provide a direct test of whether explicit discontinuity modeling adds value beyond mode switching alone.
